Starting with the digital to analog converter (DAC), unsurprisingly, when translating digital signals to analog, noise can be introduced. Firstly, good PCB design is important; separating analog and digital sections. It is also important to take into account the noise produced by clocks and power supplies. To mitigate these, good PCB design and power supply design are often good solutions. You can go into much more detail, but it can be summed up with good device selection and PCB design.

Secondly, filters can sometimes be a source of noise. Thermal noise is generated by all components, due to the resistive properties of most conductors, but resistors, having the largest resistive properties, tend to create most of the thermal noise. This could be reduced by minimising the use of resistors in filters, and paying attention to the thermal and resistive properties of filter components. Next, reflections can occur from bad filter design or impedance mismatches, which don’t necessarily introduce noise, but can increase the effect of noise by degrading the signal. Finally, all components are non-ideal, so if amplitudes are too elevated, the filter can act unexpectedly, and even distort the signal.

Thirdly, amplifiers can also be sources of noise in an RF chain. More importantly, the position of the amplifier can determine how much noise is amplified. As for reducing noise generated from amplifiers, I am starting to repeat myself: choose the correct type of amplifier, good power supply/ oscillator, good PCB design.

In reality, noise can be produced by all components in any circuit. These are only a few common component examples. To manage noise properly, the designer should have a holistic view of noise sources, in order to balance potential sources, with the downsides of these mitigation techniques. Another thing I would like to highlight is that the position of components in the chain is also important. For example, putting filters before amplifiers or after potential sources of noise, or amplifying in moderation.

##GEMINI:
In an RF TX communication chain, which components could introduce noise? How can this noise be managed?

Part 1: Components That Introduce Noise
Local Oscillators (LO) & Synthesizers (PLLs): Introduce phase noise and jitter, causing carrier frequency phase distortion and energy spilling into adjacent channels.

Power Amplifiers (PA) & Driver Amplifiers: Generate thermal noise, shot noise, and non-linear intermodulation distortion (IMD) when driven near saturation.

Mixers: Add conversion loss noise, flicker noise ($1/f$ noise at baseband), and thermal noise during upconversion.

Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs): Produce quantization noise, clock jitter, and out-of-band alias frequencies.

Power Supply & Regulators: Pass switching ripple, voltage spikes, and low-frequency noise onto RF lines due to PSRR limits, creating unwanted spurious tones.

Passive Components (Resistors, Traces): Contribute fundamental thermal (Johnson–Nyquist) noise proportional to temperature and bandwidth.

Part 2: Noise Management & Mitigation Techniques
Bandpass & Low-Pass Filtering: Place high-Q bandpass filters after mixers and PAs to eliminate out-of-band harmonics and broadband noise.

Power Supply Decoupling & Low-Noise LDOs: Use dedicated low-noise LDO regulators, ferrite beads, and decoupling capacitors on sensitive LO and VCO supply lines.

Amplifier Linearization: Implement Digital Pre-Distortion (DPD) or operate the PA with adequate power back-off to prevent spectral regrowth.

Low-Phase-Noise Clocking: Utilize high-quality TCXO/OCXO crystal reference clocks and optimize PLL loop bandwidths.

Shielding & Impedance Matching: Maintain precise impedance matching across stages and isolate RF sections with ground planes and metal shielding cans.
